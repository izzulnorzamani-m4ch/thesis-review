# Assessing a thesis statement

A thesis statement is the one- or two-sentence central claim of an essay or paper. Feedback here is short and given in the reply — no document needed.

## What to ask for

If the student only pasted the statement, it helps to know the essay prompt/question and the type of essay (argumentative, analytical, expository, compare-and-contrast) and level. Ask once if it matters; otherwise make a reasonable assumption and say what it was.

## Criteria

1. **Arguable** — a reasonable person could disagree. "Social media affects teenagers" is a fact, not a thesis. (Expository essays are the exception: a clear controlling idea is enough.)
2. **Specific** — names the particular claim, not a broad topic. Vague words ("impact", "important", "many factors", "positive and negative") usually signal a missing claim.
3. **Scoped** — provable within the essay's length; not trying to settle a whole field.
4. **Answers the prompt** — directly responds to the question asked, if there is one.
5. **Shows the reasoning (optional but strong)** — signals the main grounds ("because...", "by...", "through...") so the reader can predict the essay's structure.
6. **Clear and concise** — one main idea; no hedging so heavy that the claim disappears; no announcing ("In this essay I will discuss...").

## Output

```
**Verdict:** [Strong / Workable but needs sharpening / Not yet a thesis]

**What works:** [1–2 points]

**What to fix:** [the 1–3 most important problems, each with why]

**How to sharpen it:** [guidance on the direction to take, plus at most one or two example rewrites clearly labelled as illustrations — the student should write their own version]

**Check against your essay:** [one line reminding them the body paragraphs must each support this claim; if they shared the essay, note any paragraph that doesn't]
```

## Example

Input: "Climate change is a big problem that affects the world in many ways."

- Verdict: Not yet a thesis — it's a topic statement.
- Issues: not arguable (almost no one disputes it), not specific ("big problem", "many ways"), no scope.
- Direction: pick one effect, one place or group, and take a position someone could dispute — e.g., about which policy response works best, or who should bear the cost.
- Illustration: "Malaysia's coastal states should prioritise mangrove restoration over sea walls, because restoration protects against flooding at lower long-term cost while supporting fisheries."
