# Chapter criteria

Each section below describes a *function* a thesis has to perform, written in fairly general terms. For field-specific expectations (what counts as rigour in a lab study vs a law thesis vs a design project), pair this with `discipline-notes.md`. Map the student's actual chapters onto these functions — a humanities thesis or thesis by publication may spread them differently. Use the "check" items as prompts for your reading, not as a checklist to recite back.

## 1. Title, abstract and front matter

**Purpose:** let a reader know in two minutes what was studied, how, what was found and why it matters.

Check:
- Title is specific (topic, context, and often method/population); not a vague theme.
- Abstract covers problem, aim, method, key findings (with substance, not "results are discussed"), contribution/implications. Within the word limit.
- Abstract matches the thesis — findings and claims identical to the body. Mismatches are common after late revisions.
- Keywords, table of contents, lists of figures/tables/abbreviations are present and accurate.

## 2. Introduction

**Purpose:** establish the problem, show it matters, show what's unknown, and state precisely what this study will do.

Check:
- Background moves from broad context to a specific problem without a long general preamble.
- **Problem statement** is evidenced (data, literature, practice), not just asserted.
- **Gap** is specific and credible — "few studies in [this country/context]" is weak on its own; *why* that gap matters needs stating.
- **Aim, objectives and research questions/hypotheses** are clear, consistent with each other (each RQ maps to an objective), feasible, and answerable by the chosen method. Verbs matter: "explore", "determine", "evaluate", "develop" imply different designs.
- Significance/contribution is stated for theory and/or practice, without overclaiming.
- Scope and delimitations, operational definitions of key terms, and a thesis outline are present where the discipline expects them.

## 3. Literature review

**Purpose:** demonstrate command of the field and build the case that this study is needed.

Check:
- **Synthesis, not summary.** Organised by themes, debates or variables, comparing and contrasting sources. Paragraphs that each describe one study ("Ahmad (2019) found... Lee (2020) found...") indicate summary.
- **Critical evaluation:** strengths, weaknesses, contradictions and methodological limits of prior work are discussed.
- **Currency:** includes recent work (last ~5 years) alongside seminal sources. Flag a review whose newest sources are many years before submission.
- **Relevance and coverage:** stays focused on what the RQs need; no major body of work conspicuously absent (say so cautiously if you're inferring).
- **Leads to the gap:** the chapter ends with the gap emerging from the review, consistent with the introduction.
- Source quality: mainly peer-reviewed; grey literature and websites used appropriately.

## 4. Theoretical / conceptual framework

(May be its own chapter or part of the literature review.)

**Purpose:** provide the lens that shapes what is measured or explored and how results are interpreted.

Check:
- Theory/model is explained accurately and its choice justified over alternatives.
- Constructs/variables are defined and the relationships (hypotheses, propositions) follow logically.
- **The framework is used later** — it drives instrument design or coding and is returned to in the discussion. A framework that disappears after Chapter 2 is decorative (Major at master's, potentially Critical at PhD).
- Any framework diagram matches the text and the analysis actually performed.

## 5. Methodology

**Purpose:** show the study was designed and conducted so that its findings can be trusted.

Check across all designs:
- Research philosophy/paradigm (where the discipline expects it) is consistent with the design.
- Design is justified *in relation to the RQs*, not just described. Alternatives considered at postgraduate level.
- Population, sampling technique and sample size are stated and justified.
- Data collection procedures are reproducible in enough detail.
- Ethics: approval, consent, confidentiality, data storage.
- Limitations of the design acknowledged here or in the discussion.

Quantitative:
- Instrument source/adaptation, validity (content, construct) and reliability (e.g., Cronbach's α) reported; pilot study where appropriate.
- Sample size justified (power analysis, rule of thumb with citation, or population-based formula).
- Analysis techniques match the RQs and data type; assumptions checked (normality, multicollinearity, etc.).

Qualitative:
- Participant selection and saturation/sufficiency justified.
- Interview/observation protocols described; analysis approach named and actually followed (e.g., Braun & Clarke's phases).
- Trustworthiness addressed (credibility, transferability, dependability, confirmability) and researcher positionality where relevant.

Mixed methods:
- Named design (convergent, explanatory sequential, exploratory sequential, etc.) and a clear point and logic of integration.

Design/development (e.g., ADDIE, design science, software engineering):
- Development model justified; requirements traced to the problem; evaluation is genuine (expert review, usability testing, effectiveness measures) and not just "the system works".

## 6. Results / findings

**Purpose:** report what was found, clearly and accurately, organised around the RQs.

Check:
- Organised by RQ/hypothesis, so each can be traced.
- Descriptive information on the sample/participants before inferential results or themes.
- Statistics reported completely and correctly (test statistic, df, p, effect size, CIs where appropriate); tables and figures numbered, titled, referred to in text, and not duplicating each other.
- Qualitative themes supported by sufficient, well-chosen quotations with participant codes; themes are analytic, not just topic headings.
- Results are reported, not interpreted at length (unless a combined Results & Discussion chapter is used deliberately).
- Numbers are internally consistent (n, percentages, totals across tables).

## 7. Discussion

**Purpose:** explain what the findings mean and how they relate to existing knowledge.

Check:
- Each key finding interpreted, not restated.
- Findings compared with prior studies from the literature review — agreements and disagreements explained.
- Unexpected or non-significant findings addressed honestly.
- Returns to the theoretical framework: what the results say about the theory.
- Theoretical, practical and (where relevant) policy implications are specific and actually follow from the findings.
- Limitations are substantive (how they affect interpretation), not a token list.
- No overclaiming: causal language only for causal designs; generalisation matched to the sample.

## 8. Conclusion

**Purpose:** close the argument.

Check:
- Explicitly answers each RQ / states whether each objective was achieved.
- States the contribution clearly (essential at PhD).
- Recommendations for practice and for future research follow from the findings and limitations.
- No new data, literature or arguments introduced.
- Ends with a concise closing statement rather than trailing off.

## 9. References, citation and academic integrity

Check:
- Consistent use of the required style (APA, Harvard, IEEE, etc.).
- In-text citations and reference list correspond (spot-check several in both directions).
- Secondary citing ("as cited in") kept to a minimum.
- Signs of possible problems to flag gently for the student to verify: references you can't plausibly match to real works, wrong years for well-known works, long passages that read very differently from the rest of the text, quotations without page numbers.
- Over-reliance on a few sources, or on non-scholarly websites.

## 10. Writing, presentation and formatting

Check:
- Academic register; clear, concise sentences; paragraphs with one main idea.
- Signposting: chapter introductions and summaries, transitions that carry the argument.
- Consistent terminology and abbreviations (defined on first use).
- Tense conventions for the discipline.
- Figures/tables legible, labelled, and sourced.
- Appendices contain instruments, consent forms, ethics approval and supplementary data as appropriate.

Report language issues by pattern with a few examples. Reserve Major ratings for language that obscures meaning or appears across the whole thesis.

## 11. Non-standard structures

- **Thesis by publication:** check the introductory and concluding chapters (exegesis) carefully — they must establish the overarching problem, show how the papers connect, and synthesise the contribution. Check the student's contribution to co-authored papers is stated. Repetition across papers is normal but should be managed.
- **Humanities / argument-led theses:** methodology may be a section on approach and sources; literature may be woven through chapters. Judge the strength and originality of the argument, quality of close reading or archival work, engagement with critical debates, and the coherence of the chapter sequence.
- **Practice-based / creative theses:** check the relationship between the creative/practical component and the exegesis, and that research questions are answered through practice and reflection.
